# Start here

The Technician license is the front door to ham radio. Pass it and you can talk through repeaters across town, bounce signals off satellites, run digital modes like FT8 on 6 m and 2 m, and make HF contacts around the planet on 10 m, 15 m, 40 m, 80 m using Morse code, and on 10 m voice and data.

**The exam in one paragraph.** 35 multiple-choice questions, drawn from a public pool of 409 questions. You need **26 correct (74%)** to pass. The pool is public: every question you will see is in the appendix of this PDF, with the answer. This is not a trick exam. It rewards people who have seen the questions before and understand *why* the answer is right.

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Questions on exam | 35 |
| Passing score | 26 correct (74%) |
| Pool size | 409 questions, 10 subelements, 35 groups |
| How the exam is built | One question is drawn from each of the 35 groups (T1A, T1B, …, T0C) |
| Cost | A small VE session fee, commonly about \$15 (varies by team). The FCC application fee was eliminated in 2022. |
| Before exam day | Register for a free **FCC Registration Number (FRN)** at the FCC CORES site. You enter it on the exam form. Do not skip this. |
| Where | Local VE sessions (find via ARRL, W5YI/VEC sites, or HamStudy) or an online remote session |
| After you pass | The FCC emails you a link to download your license grant. You may transmit as soon as your license appears in the FCC's database (ULS). |

**Why "one question from each group" matters.** You can see exactly which groups give you the most reliable points. Groups T5 (electrical math) and T6 (components) are pure memorization once you learn five formulas and the symbol shapes. Take those points first.

## A study plan that works

* **Days 1 to 3.** Read this guide start to finish. Do not memorize yet. Just get the lay of the land.
* **Days 4 to 10.** Open the web app (`site/`) or any practice tool. Drill one subelement per day, in *Learn* mode, until you stay above 80%.
* **Days 11 to 13.** Take full 35-question exam simulations. Review every miss. Aim for 30/35 or better on three runs in a row.
* **Day before.** Skim the "Cram sheet" at the end of this guide. Sleep. Bring photo ID, your FRN, a pencil, and a simple calculator (check your session's rules).

> **The honest rule of thumb.** If you get 85% or better on three fresh simulations, you will pass. Real exam stress costs you two or three questions, so build a cushion.

# T1 · FCC Rules and the Basics (6 exam questions)

> **Elmer says:** Rules feel like trivia until you spot the pattern. Nearly every question is *who is responsible*, *who may*, *how often*, or *how much*. Part 97 is the rulebook, and every question cites the section it came from. Under each heading below you will find the official syllabus topic and the rule sections behind it, with links to the full text on ecfr.gov. (In the PDF the same lines appear without links.)

This is the biggest subelement (68 pool questions) and the one people hate most, because it is rules. Learn the numbers.

## T1A · Purpose of the service, who regulates it

* The FCC regulates and enforces amateur radio in the U.S. The rules are **Part 97** of Title 47 of the Code of Federal Regulations.
* The **basis and purpose** (§97.1) includes: public service (emergency communications), advancing the radio art, advancing communication and technical skills, expanding the pool of trained operators, and international goodwill. Contesting is a legitimate activity, but the pool's answer is **"advancing skills in the technical and communication phases of the radio art."**
* **Phonetic alphabet** use for identification is *encouraged* with phone emissions, not required.
* After passing, the FCC emails a link to download your license grant. You are legal when the license appears in the FCC ULS database.
* A **space station** is an amateur station located more than 50 km above Earth's surface.
* **Beacons** (automatic propagation beacons) on HF live on **10 m between 28.200 and 28.300 MHz**.
* Repeaters and auxiliary stations get their channels from a **Volunteer Frequency Coordinator** recognized by local amateurs. That coordinator is made up of amateurs in the local or regional area whose stations are eligible to be repeaters or auxiliary stations.
* To be a RACES control operator you need your FCC license plus **certification of current enrollment by a civil defense organization**.
* **Prohibited:** willful or malicious interference.

## T1B · Frequencies and power: the part to memorize

**Band names and the wavelength trick.** The band name is roughly the wavelength in meters. 146.52 MHz is in the **2 meter** band (144 to 148 MHz). 52.525 MHz is in the **6 meter** band (50 to 54 MHz).

| Band | Range | Notes for a Technician |
|---|---|---|
| 6 m | 50 – 54 MHz | CW-only 50.0–50.1 MHz. SSB and digital allowed. |
| 2 m | 144 – 148 MHz | CW-only 144.0–144.1 MHz. The workhorse band. 146.52 MHz is the national simplex calling frequency. |
| 1.25 m | 222 – 225 MHz | |
| 70 cm | 420 – 450 MHz | |
| 33 cm / 23 cm | 902–928 / 1240–1300 MHz | |

**Technician HF privileges (this is the exam favorite):**

| Band | Technician privilege |
|---|---|
| 80 m, 40 m, 15 m | CW only (Morse), 200 W PEP |
| 10 m | **28.000–28.300 MHz** CW and data (RTTY/data) ; **28.300–28.500 MHz** voice (SSB) |

* Technician has **no phone privileges on any HF band except 10 m**. On the pool question: *"On which HF bands does a Technician have phone privileges?"* → **10-meter band only**. The phone segment is **28.300 to 28.500 MHz**.
* HF power limit for Technicians: **200 watts PEP**.
* Above 30 MHz (VHF/UHF): **1500 watts PEP**, with exceptions.
* **Digital modes like FT8** are allowed where you have privileges. The pool names 10 m, 6 m and 2 m (all three are correct).
* **Above 50 MHz, SSB is allowed in at least some segment of all those bands.** (You are not limited to repeaters.)
* Where the amateur service is *secondary*, you may find non-amateur stations and you must avoid interfering with them.
* **Do not transmit exactly on a band or sub-band edge.** The pool lists three reasons, all correct: transmitter frequency drift, calibration error in the frequency display, and modulation sidebands that would extend beyond the edge. Stay inside by a safe margin.

> **Elmer says:** Why is a *power limit* in the "rules" subelement? Because it **is** a rule. §97.313 (transmitter power standards) caps your power, §97.301 says where you may operate, and §97.305 says which modes. T1B is the group for "where, what mode, how much power", which is why a question like "maximum PEP for a Technician on HF" (answer: 200 W) cites §97.313.

## T1C · Licenses, call signs, renewals, vanity

* The FCC currently issues new licenses in three classes: **Technician, General, Amateur Extra.** (Novice, Technician Plus and Advanced are grandfathered: they can renew but no new ones are issued.)
* License term: **10 years.** Renew **up to 90 days before** expiration.
* **Grace period: 2 years** after expiration to renew, but **you may NOT transmit** until it is renewed. After 2 years, you start over with a new exam.
* **Vanity call signs:** any licensed amateur can request one.
* The FCC contacts you by email. If it cannot reach you, the penalty is **revocation of the station license or suspension of the operator license.** Keep your email current in the FCC database.
* **International communications** are permitted if they are incidental to the purposes of the service and are remarks of a personal character.
* Operating aboard a vessel in international waters: you may operate from a **U.S.-documented vessel with the master's permission** (the pool's answer).
* Valid new call sign format example: **KF1XXX** (a two-letter prefix, one digit, three letters).

## T1D · What you may not do

* **Never** broadcast. Broadcasting means transmissions intended for reception by the **general public**. One-way transmissions are prohibited except for specific things (beacons, code practice, bulletins, remote control of model craft, etc.).
* **No encrypted messages** to obscure their meaning, with two exceptions: control commands to **space stations** or **model craft**. (Approved digital codes are *not* "encryption"; they have published specs.)
* **Music** may be transmitted only when it is incidental to an authorized retransmission of manned spacecraft communications.
* **Selling gear:** you may announce your own equipment for sale only when it is not done on a regular basis. You can never run a business on the bands.
* **No pay** for operating, with one big exception: classroom instruction at an educational institution.
* **Obscene or indecent language is prohibited.**
* **News gathering or broadcast support** is permitted only when it is directly related to the immediate safety of human life or protection of property, and no other means is available.
* **Identification:** you must always identify your station. You can only skip callsign ID when transmitting signals to **control model craft**.
* **Test transmissions** must identify the transmitting station.
* **Countries:** you may not exchange communications with any country whose administration has notified the ITU that it objects to such communications.
* An **auxiliary station** is a station sending one-way transmissions between a remote repeater receiver and the main repeater transmitter, or a remote link station.

## T1E · Control operators and station control

* A **control operator** is the licensed person designated by the station licensee to be responsible for transmissions and compliance with the rules.
* The **station licensee designates** the control operator.
* **Your privileges follow the control operator's license class**, not the highest license on the premises and not the station owner's.
* A station may **never** transmit without a control operator. (Even auto-control stations, such as repeaters, have one.)
* **Control point:** the location where the control operator function is performed.
* **Automatic control** example: repeater operation.
* **Remote control** example: operating the station over the internet (this includes SDRs run remotely).
* If the control operator is not the licensee, **both the control operator and the station licensee** are responsible for proper operation.
* A Technician may be the control operator of a station in an Amateur Extra band segment **at no time** (the pool's answer, even at a club station), apart from the emergency exception in T2C09.
* **Satellite control operator:** any amateur allowed to transmit on the satellite uplink frequency.

> **Elmer says:** Two sections do most of the work in T1E. **§97.103** is about the *station licensee*, the person who holds the station license: the licensee designates the control operator (§97.103(b)) and shares responsibility for the station (§97.103(a)). **§97.105** lists the control operator's duties. The idea to carry into the exam: *your privileges follow the person at the controls, not the person who owns the radio.* The pool cites §97.103(b) for both "who designates the control operator" and "what sets the transmitting privileges".

## T1F · Station ID, third-party, clubs

* **Identify** at the end of a communication and **at least every 10 minutes** during it.
* **Phone ID:** send the call sign using either CW or phone, in **English**.
* **Self-assigned indicators** like "KL7CC slash W3" are acceptable. Slash, stroke and slant are all accepted (pool: all three).
* **Tactical call signs** ("Race Headquarters") still require you to give your FCC call sign at least every 10 minutes and at the end.
* **Third-party communications:** a message from a control operator to another amateur station control operator **on behalf of another person.** When an unlicensed person speaks to a foreign amateur station, the **foreign station must be in a country with which the U.S. has a third-party agreement.**
* A **repeater station** simultaneously retransmits the signal of another amateur station on a different channel.
* **If a repeater retransmits a rule-breaking signal**, the **control operator of the originating station** is accountable (not the repeater owner).
* **Inspection:** make the station and records available **at any time upon request by an FCC representative.**
* **Club station license:** the club must have **at least four members.**

# T2 · Operating Procedures (3 exam questions)

> **Elmer says:** A repeater is a radio on a tall tower that listens on one frequency and re-transmits on another at the same time. That is why your 5 W handheld can reach across a city: the hard part (height) is done for you. Everything in T2 is about being a good guest on someone else's repeater.

<!-- img:repeater A tower like this carries repeater and link antennas. Height is range. -->

## T2A · Repeaters, calling, band plans

* **Repeater offset** = the difference between the repeater's transmit and receive frequencies.
* **2 m offset: ± 600 kHz.** **70 cm offset: ± 5 MHz.** (Students always flip these. Memory hook: the lower band gets the smaller offset, VHF 600 kHz, UHF 5 MHz.)
* **National FM simplex calling frequency on 2 m: 146.520 MHz.** (70 cm: 446.000 MHz.)
* **Simplex** = transmitting and receiving on the same frequency.
* **Calling a specific station on a repeater:** say the other station's call sign, then yours.
* **Answering a CQ:** give the other station's call sign, then yours.
* **CQ** means "calling any station."
* **Showing you're available on a repeater:** say your **call sign followed by the word "listening."** (Not "CQ CQ" the way you would on HF.)
* **Seeking a contact outside a repeater:** call "CQ" a few times, "this is," your call sign, then pause to listen.
* A **band plan** is a voluntary guideline for using different modes or activities within a band.

## T2B · Radio operation

* **Reverse (REV) function:** lets you **listen on the repeater's input frequency** (to see whether you can hear the other station directly, so you can switch to simplex).
* **CTCSS** is the continuous sub-audible tone that opens a repeater's squelch. **DCS** is the digital-coded version. **DTMF** is the touch-tone pad (two simultaneous tones).
* **Can't access a repeater you can hear?** Improper offset, wrong CTCSS tone, or wrong DCS code can each keep you out (the pool lists all three).
* **FM audio drops out on peaks:** you are talking too loudly (over-deviation).
* **Two stations on the same frequency interfere:** negotiate continued use of the frequency.
* **Why simplex channels exist:** so stations within range of each other can talk **without tying up a repeater**.
* **Q signals:**

| Q signal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| QRM | I'm being interfered with (man-made) |
| QRN | Static / natural noise |
| QSB | Fading |
| QSY | Change frequency |
| QTH | My location |
| QRZ | Who is calling me? |
| QSL | I confirm / received |
| QRP | Low power |

* **Squelch** mutes the receiver audio when no signal is present.
* **Linked repeater network:** signals received by one repeater are transmitted by all the repeaters in the network.
* **Digital repeater talkgroups (DMR, etc.):** program your radio with the **group's ID or code**.
* **DMR color code:** an access code programmed into the radio to reach a specific repeater.
* A **talkgroup** is an identifier used by DMR to organize traffic so people who want to hear a group aren't bothered by those who don't.

## T2C · Public service and emergency

* **FCC rules always apply**, even during ARES activity.
* **RACES** is an FCC Part 97 service for **civil defense communications during national emergencies**. It requires civil-defense certification.
* **ARES** is a group of licensed amateurs who have **voluntarily registered** their qualifications and equipment for emergency communications. No federal certification.
* **Net control station (NCS):** calls the net to order and directs communications between stations checking in.
* In a net, **transmit only when directed by the NCS**, unless you are reporting an emergency.
* **Traffic** = formal messages exchanged by net stations.
* **Spell unusual words** with a standard **phonetic alphabet**.
* A formal **message preamble** holds information needed to track the message. **"Check"** = the number of words or word equivalents in the text.
* **Winlink** relays messages using email addresses based on amateur callsigns.
* During an emergency with immediate threat to human life or property, a control operator may operate outside their license privileges.

**ITU phonetic alphabet:** Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, Golf, Hotel, India, Juliett, Kilo, Lima, Mike, November, Oscar, Papa, Quebec, Romeo, Sierra, Tango, Uniform, Victor, Whiskey, X-ray, Yankee, Zulu.

# T3 · Radio Wave Characteristics (3 exam questions)

> **Elmer says:** Picture shaking a rope. How fast you shake is the **frequency**, the distance between humps is the **wavelength**, and the speed of the wave is fixed (the speed of light). Shake faster and the humps get closer together. That one picture answers half of T3.

## T3A · Signal fading and polarization

* **Why VHF signals change a lot when you move the antenna a few feet:** **multipath** cancels or reinforces signals.
* **Vegetation** absorbs UHF/microwave signals, giving poor reception of weak signals.
* **Polarization** is the orientation of the **electric field**. Vertical antennas for FM repeaters, horizontal for weak-signal SSB/CW on VHF/UHF.
* **Cross-polarized** antennas = received signal strength drops.
* **Picket fencing:** rapid flutter on mobile signals from multipath.
* **Rain and precipitation** reduce range at microwave frequencies. **Fog and rain have little effect on 10 m and 6 m.**
* **Irregular ionospheric fading:** random combining of signals arriving via different paths.
* **Multipath on data transmissions:** **error rates are likely to increase.**
* The **ionosphere** reflects (refracts) HF radio waves. (Not the troposphere.)
* Because ionospheric signals have elliptical polarization, either vertical or horizontal antennas work.

* **Reflections change things.** A signal bouncing off a building or hillside can arrive at a different polarization and strength. If your directional antenna points straight at a blocked repeater, try pointing at something that reflects the signal toward it (the pool's answer for a blocked path).

<!-- lab:polar -->

## T3B · The wave itself

* Electric and magnetic fields are **at right angles**.
* Components: **electric and magnetic fields.**
* Radio waves travel at the **speed of light**, **300,000,000 meters per second** in free space. All radio frequencies travel at the same speed.
* **Wavelength gets shorter as frequency increases.**
* **Wavelength (m) = 300 ÷ frequency (MHz).** So 146 MHz → ≈ 2 m. 7 MHz → ≈ 43 m.
* **Frequency ranges:**

| Name | Range |
|---|---|
| HF | 3 – 30 MHz |
| VHF | 30 – 300 MHz |
| UHF | 300 – 3000 MHz |

* A band name (like "2 meters") gives the **approximate wavelength in meters**.

<!-- lab:wave -->

## T3C · Propagation modes

* **Line of sight** is the usual story for VHF/UHF. UHF signals are usually not propagated by the ionosphere.
* **Radio horizon > visual horizon** because the atmosphere **refracts** radio waves slightly.
* **Knife-edge diffraction** lets signals bend over obstructions.
* **Sporadic E:** the cause of occasional strong signals on **10 m, 6 m, and 2 m** from beyond the horizon.
* **Tropospheric ducting:** regular over-the-horizon VHF/UHF to about 300 miles. Caused by **temperature inversions** in the atmosphere.
* **Meteor scatter:** best on **6 m**.
* **Auroral backscatter:** signals are **distorted with a raspy sound.**
* **10 m F-layer propagation:** best **from dawn to shortly after sunset during high sunspot activity.** At the sunspot peak, **6 m and 10 m** open for long-distance F-region contacts.
* **HF** supports more **long-distance ionospheric propagation** than VHF.

# T4 · Amateur Radio Practices (2 exam questions)

<!-- img:handheld A handheld transceiver. Most controls in T4B (squelch, memory, scan, tone) live on a radio like this. -->

## T4A · Station setup

* **Mobile 50 W FM radio power supply:** **13.8 V at 12 A** (a typical 12 V car system).
* **Short, heavy-gauge wire** for DC power: **minimize voltage drop** when transmitting.
* **RF power meter / SWR meter** goes **in the feed line between transmitter and antenna.**
* **SWR meter** selection: consider the **frequency and power** at which measurements will be made.
* **Digital mode station (FT8):** computer audio **output and input** connect to the transceiver. A **computer-radio interface** carries **receive audio, transmit audio, and transmitter keying (PTT).**
* **Bonding at RF:** prefer **flat copper strap**.
* **Battery run time** = battery **amp-hour rating ÷ average current draw.**
* **Mobile ground:** negative return to the **12 V battery chassis ground**.
* **Electronic keyer:** a device that assists in manual sending of Morse code.
* **Digital hotspot:** gives nearby radios communication with a digital voice or data network.
* **Computer to radio, audio side:** the pool answer is computer "line in" to the transceiver's speaker connector (receive audio goes in), and your computer's audio output feeds the radio's microphone or data input. Keying (PTT) is a third, separate connection.

## T4B · Operating a transceiver

* **Excessive mic gain on SSB:** distorted audio.
* **Entering a frequency:** keypad **or** VFO knob.
* **Squelch for weak FM signals:** the pool's answer is to set the squelch threshold so receiver output audio is **on all the time** (open it, so a weak signal is not muted).
* **Off-frequency FM:** distorted audio.
* **Scanning:** tunes through a range of frequencies to check for activity.
* **SSB pitch too high/low from the other station?** Use **RIT / clarifier.**
* **DMR code plug:** configuration data loaded onto your radio for repeaters and talkgroups.
* **Filter bandwidth choice:** narrower for CW, ~**2400 Hz** for SSB, so a bandwidth matched to the mode cuts noise and interference.
* **DMR group:** you pick a talkgroup by entering the group's identification code.
* **D-STAR** radios need your **call sign** programmed in before transmitting (plus repeater and routing info).

# T5 · Electrical Principles (4 exam questions)

> **Elmer says:** Water pipe analogy. **Voltage** is the water pressure, **current** is how much water flows, and **resistance** is how narrow the pipe is. More pressure means more flow; a narrower pipe means less flow. Ohm's law is just that sentence in algebra. **Power** is how hard the water is working: pressure times flow.

**The most dependable points on the exam.** Learn these cold.

## T5A · Basic terms

| Quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| Voltage (EMF), the push | volt | E or V |
| Current, the flow | ampere | I |
| Resistance, the opposition | ohm | R |
| Power, the rate of using energy | watt | P |
| Frequency | hertz | f |
| Capacitance | farad | C |
| Inductance | henry | L |
| Impedance | ohm | Z |

* **A difference in voltage** causes electron flow (current).
* **Conductors** (metals): many free electrons. **Insulators**: glass, plastic.
* **AC** alternates between positive and negative directions. **DC** flows one way. **Frequency** = cycles per second.
* Resistance opposes **all** current: direct current, alternating current, and RF current.

## T5B · Prefixes and decibels

| Prefix | Symbol | Multiplier |
|---|---|---|
| pico | p | 10⁻¹² |
| nano | n | 10⁻⁹ |
| micro | µ | 10⁻⁶ |
| milli | m | 10⁻³ |
| kilo | k | 10³ |
| mega | M | 10⁶ |
| giga | G | 10⁹ |

* 1.5 A = **1500 mA.** 500 mW = **0.5 W.** 3000 mA = **3 A.**
* 1,500,000 Hz = **1500 kHz.** 3.525 MHz = **3525 kHz.** 28400 kHz = **28.400 MHz.** 2425 MHz = **2.425 GHz.**
* 1,000,000 pF = **1 µF.**

**Decibels, the cheat:**

| Change | dB |
|---|---|
| double the power | **+3 dB** |
| half the power | **−3 dB** |
| 4× power | **+6 dB** |
| ¼ power | **−6 dB** |
| 10× power | **+10 dB** |

5 W → 10 W = **+3 dB.** 12 W → 3 W = **−6 dB** (÷4). 20 W → 200 W = **+10 dB** (×10).

<!-- lab:db -->

## T5C · Capacitance, inductance, and power

* **Capacitance** stores energy in an **electric field**. Unit: **farad.**
* **Inductance** stores energy in a **magnetic field**. Unit: **henry.**
* **Impedance** = opposition to AC current flow. Unit: **ohm.**
* **Power: P = I × E** (watts = amps × volts).
  * 13.8 V × 10 A = **138 W.**
  * 12 V × 2.5 A = **30 W.**
  * Current for 120 W at 12 V: I = P/E = **10 A.**

## T5D · Ohm's law

```
        E            E = I × R   (voltage)
     -------         I = E / R   (current)
      I × R          R = E / I   (resistance)
```

* 90 V, 3 A → R = **30 Ω.** 12 V, 1.5 A → **8 Ω.** 12 V, 4 A → **3 Ω.**
* 120 V across 80 Ω → **1.5 A.** 200 V across 100 Ω → **2 A.** 240 V across 24 Ω → **10 A.**
* 0.5 A through 2 Ω → **1 V.** 1 A through 10 Ω → **10 V.** 2 A through 10 Ω → **20 V.**
* **Series circuit:** current is the **same** through every component.
* **Parallel circuit:** voltage is the **same** across every component.

> **Tip.** Write E = I × R on your scratch paper the moment the exam starts. Cover the letter you want to find. What's left tells you the operation.

# T6 · Electrical Components (4 exam questions)

> **Elmer says:** Meet the cast. **Resistor** limits current. **Capacitor** is a tiny rechargeable bucket of electric field. **Inductor** is a coil that resists changes in current by storing magnetic field. **Diode** is a one-way valve. **Transistor** is a valve you control with a small signal. Once you know what each *does*, the symbols stop being random squiggles.

<!-- img:components A pile of real parts: resistors, capacitors and diodes. Learn to spot them. -->

## T6A · Components and switches, batteries

| Component | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Resistor** | opposes current flow in a DC circuit |
| **Potentiometer** | variable resistor, used as volume control (controls **resistance**) |
| **Capacitor** | stores energy in an electric field; two conductive surfaces separated by an insulator |
| **Inductor** | stores energy in a magnetic field; typically a coil of wire |
| **SPDT switch** | switches a single circuit between one of two other circuits |
| **SPST switch** | single-pole single-throw (just on/off) |

* **Rechargeable chemistries:** lithium-ion, NiMH, NiCd, lead-acid. **Not rechargeable:** carbon-zinc (and alkaline primary).

## T6B · Semiconductors

* **Diode:** current in **one direction** only. Electrodes: **anode and cathode.** The cathode is marked with a **stripe.** Forward voltage drop is lower in some diode types (Schottky, germanium) than in others.
* **LED** emits light when **forward current** flows through it.
* **Transistor:** electronic switch and amplifier; can provide **power gain.** Three regions of semiconductor.
  * **Bipolar (BJT):** **emitter, base, collector.**
  * **FET (field-effect transistor):** **gate, drain, source.**
* **Gain** in amplifiers is the output signal **voltage** relative to input voltage, **current** relative to input current, or **power** relative to input power. The pool lists all three.

## T6C · Schematics

* A **schematic** uses standard symbols to show how components connect. It accurately represents **component connections** (not physical size or position).
* Figures T-1, T-2, T-3 are in the figures folder (and in the PDF appendix). Learn the shapes: resistor (zig-zag), capacitor (two plates), inductor (coils), transistor, battery (long/short lines), lamp (circle with an X), LED (diode with arrows), transformer (two coils with parallel lines), variable resistor (resistor with arrow), variable inductor, antenna (Y shape).
* From the pool: figure T-1 component 1 = resistor, 2 = transistor, 3 = lamp, 4 = battery. T-2 component 4 = transformer, 6 = capacitor, 8 = LED, 9 = variable resistor. T-3 component 3 = variable inductor, 4 = antenna. T-2 component 3 = SPST switch.

<!-- lab:symbols -->

## T6D · Other components

* **Rectifier:** changes AC into varying DC.
* **Relay:** an electrically controlled switch.
* **Shielded wire** prevents coupling of unwanted signals to or from the wire.
* **Meter** displays an electrical quantity as a number.
* **Regulator** controls the voltage from a power supply.
* **Transformer** changes 120 V AC to a lower AC voltage.
* **Resonant (tuned) circuit:** an **inductor and capacitor** in series or parallel.
* **Integrated circuit (IC):** several semiconductors and other parts in one package.

# T7 · Station Equipment (4 exam questions)

> **Elmer says:** Think of a receiver as a funnel: the antenna catches everything, filters pick out the one signal you want (**selectivity**), amplifiers make weak ones audible (**sensitivity**), and a **mixer** slides the signal to a frequency that is easier to work with. A transmitter runs the same chain backwards. Most "problems" in T7B come from a signal getting into a place it should not.

## T7A · Transmitters, receivers, transceivers

* **Sensitivity** = ability to detect a weak signal. **Selectivity** = ability to separate signals.
* **Transceiver** = receiver + transmitter in one box.
* **Mixer** converts a signal from one frequency to another. **Oscillator** creates a signal at a set frequency. **Transverter** converts the RF in/out to another band.
* **PTT** (push-to-talk) switches the radio from receive to transmit when grounded.
* **Modulation** = combining speech (information) with an RF carrier.
* **VFO** sets the transmit and receive frequency.
* **RF power amplifier** boosts output. An SSB / CW-FM switch on a VHF amp sets the amplifier for proper operation in the selected mode.

## T7B · Interference and distortion

* **Over-deviating FM:** talk farther from the microphone.
* **Broadcast radios hearing you:** the receiver is unable to reject strong signals outside its intended band. Fix with a filter at the affected receiver's antenna input.
* **Radio frequency interference** can come from fundamental overload, harmonics, and spurious emissions (the pool lists all three).
* **Low power out of a solid-state transceiver:** **high SWR** (the radio folds back power).
* **A neighbor says you're causing TV interference:** make sure your own station is working properly and not creating interference.
* **Cable TV interference first step:** make sure all TV feed-line coax connectors are installed properly.
* **Nearby commercial FM interfering with your 2 m rig:** install a **band-reject filter.**
* **A neighbor's device is interfering with you (all three are right):** work with your neighbor to identify the offending device, politely inform them that FCC rules prohibit devices that cause interference, and make sure your own station meets good amateur practice.
* **Distorted audio through a repeater (all three are possible causes):** your transmitter is slightly off frequency, you are speaking too loudly or too close to the microphone, or you are in a bad location.
* **Distorted voice:** a clip-on **ferrite choke** on the mic cable to keep RF out.

## T7C · Antenna systems and testing

* **Dummy load:** prevents transmitting signals over the air during tests. A typical load is a **50 Ω non-inductive resistor mounted on a heat sink.**
* **Antenna analyzer:** determines whether an antenna is resonant at the operating frequency.
* **SWR 1:1** = perfect match. **4:1** = impedance mismatch.
* Transmitters **fold back** power at high SWR to protect the output transistors.
* **Power lost in a feed line** becomes **heat.**
* **Directional wattmeter** measures SWR.
* **Coax failures:** moisture contamination. Use **UV-resistant jackets**; UV light damages the jacket and lets water in.
* **Foam-dielectric coax** has **less loss per foot** than solid-dielectric.

## T7D · Test equipment and soldering

<!-- img:multimeter A basic multimeter. Red lead, black lead, and a dial that decides what you are measuring. -->

* **Voltmeter** measures electric potential and connects **in parallel.**
* **Ammeter** measures current and connects **in series.**
* **Ohmmeter** applies a small current and measures the resulting voltage. Measure resistance only with **power off**.
* **Multimeter** measures **voltage, resistance** (and current).

# T8 · Modulation Modes and Activities (4 exam questions)

> **Elmer says:** Every mode is a trade between **bandwidth** (how much spectrum you use) and **toughness** (how well you survive noise). CW is skinny and tough. SSB is efficient for voice. FM sounds great and takes the most room. Digital modes like FT8 give up speed to dig signals out of the noise.

## T8A · Modulation

| Mode | Facts |
|---|---|
| **SSB** | a form of amplitude modulation; **~3 kHz** bandwidth; narrower than FM; used for long-distance (weak signal) VHF/UHF; **upper sideband** on 10 m, VHF, UHF |
| **FM / PM** | repeaters and VHF packet; **10 to 15 kHz** bandwidth on repeaters; only one signal at a time can be received |
| **CW** | narrowest bandwidth, **~150 Hz** |
| **Fast-scan TV (analog)** | **~6 MHz** wide |

## T8B · Satellites

<!-- img:iss The International Space Station, which carries an amateur radio station. -->

* **Beacon:** a satellite transmission with **health and status** telemetry.
* **Anyone** may receive amateur satellite telemetry.
* **Tracking programs** show a map of the satellite's real-time position, the time, azimuth and elevation of the start, maximum and end of a pass, and the apparent frequency including Doppler shift (the pool lists all three). The input is **Keplerian elements.**
* **Doppler shift:** an observed change in frequency caused by relative motion between the satellite and your station.
* **U/V mode:** uplink on **70 cm**, downlink on **2 m.**
* **LEO** = Low Earth Orbit, period about **100 minutes.**
* **Spin fading:** the satellite and its antennas rotate.
* **Too much power on the uplink** blocks other users. Your downlink signal should be about as strong as the beacon.
* Common satellite modes: FM, SSB, CW, digital. (all of these)

## T8C · Operating activities

* **Radio direction finding** locates noise sources and hidden transmitters ("fox hunting"). Use a **directional antenna**.
* **Contesting:** contacting as many stations as possible in a time window. Send the minimum information needed.
* **Grid locator:** a letter-number designator for a geographic location (Maidenhead).
* **IRLP** connects repeaters over the internet and is accessed over the air with **DTMF**. **EchoLink** needs you to register your call sign and provide proof of license.
* **VoIP** = voice over the internet.
* **Gateway:** a station that connects other amateur stations to the internet.

## T8D · Digital modes

* **FT8** is a digital mode for low signal-to-noise operation (part of the **WSJT-X** suite).
* **APRS** can carry GPS position data, text messages, and weather data, shown on a map for tactical real-time digital communications.
* **PSK** = Phase Shift Keying.
* **NTSC** = analog fast-scan color TV.
* **DMR:** time-multiplexing **two** digital voice signals on a **12.5 kHz** channel.
* **Packet radio** includes a header with the call sign of the station the data is for, a checksum that permits error detection, and automatic repeat request (ARQ) when there is an error. The pool lists all three.
* **Digital modes (pool: all of these):** packet radio, IEEE 802.11 (Wi-Fi based mesh), and FT8.
* **WSJT-X** software supports Earth-Moon-Earth (EME), weak-signal propagation beacons, and meteor scatter.
* **CW** = Morse code.
* **ARQ:** error correction; the receiving station detects errors and asks the sender to retransmit.
* **Mesh network:** amateur data network using commercial Wi-Fi hardware with modified firmware.

# T9 · Antennas and Feed Lines (2 exam questions)

> **Elmer says:** An antenna is a transducer between wires and waves, and a good one is *resonant*: its length matches the wavelength so energy radiates instead of bouncing back to the radio. The feed line is the road between them. A bad road (loss, bad connectors, water) wastes power before it ever reaches the antenna.

<!-- img:yagi A Yagi beam. The shorter elements in front are directors; the longer one behind is the reflector. -->

## T9A · Antennas

* **Beam (Yagi):** concentrates signal in one direction, highest gain.
* **Loading:** electrically lengthening an antenna with inductors (the standard "rubber duck" trick).
* **Polarization** = orientation of the **electric field.**
* **A dipole radiates strongest broadside** to the antenna.
* **Shortening a dipole raises its resonant frequency.** (Shorter wire = higher frequency.)
* **19 inches** on 2 m is a **quarter wave** vertical.
* **5/8-wave** whip has **more gain** than a 1/4 wave.
* **Handheld rubber-duck antenna:** low efficiency. Inside a car with no outside antenna: signal reduced by the vehicle's shielding effect.
* **Antenna gain:** increase in signal strength in a given direction compared with a reference antenna.

## T9B · Feed lines and SWR

<!-- img:pl259 A PL-259 (UHF) connector, common on HF and VHF gear. -->

<!-- img:bnc A BNC connector, common on handhelds and test gear. -->

<!-- img:ladderline Open-wire (ladder) line has the lowest loss of all, but needs a tuner and care in routing. -->

* **Coax impedance:** **50 Ω** is the amateur standard.
* **Coax** is easy to use and requires few special installation considerations.
* **Antenna tuner:** matches the antenna system's impedance to the transceiver's output.
* **Loss increases as frequency increases** in coax.
* **Connectors:** **PL-259 (UHF)** at HF/VHF. **Type N** above ~400 MHz. Carefully **tape outdoor connectors** against weather.
* **Sources of loss in coax (all three):** water intrusion into connectors, high SWR, and multiple connectors in the line.
* **Connectors to tape for weather protection (all three):** PL-259, BNC, and Type N.
* **Erratic SWR:** loose connection in the antenna or feed line.
* **RG-213** has less loss than **RG-58**. **Air-insulated hardline** has the lowest loss.
* **SWR:** a measure of how well a load is matched to a transmission line.

# T0 · Safety (3 exam questions)

> **Elmer says:** The hobby's real dangers are boring and avoidable: electricity, falling from a tower, and RF energy. Learn the habits once. Three-wire cords, one common ground, discharge the big capacitors, never climb alone, and keep antennas away from power lines and people.

## T0A · Electrical safety

* **12 V battery:** shorting the terminals can cause burns, fire, or an explosion. Rapidly charging or discharging an unprotected battery risks **overheating or out-gassing.**
* **Current through the body** can injure by heating body tissue, disrupt the electrical function of cells, and cause involuntary muscle contractions (the pool lists all three).
* **Guarding against shock (all three are right):** use three-wire cords and plugs for all AC equipment, connect all AC equipment to a common safety ground, and make sure high-voltage DC capacitors are fully discharged before working inside equipment.
* **Black wire** in 120 V AC = **hot.**
* **Fuse** removes power on overload. **Never replace a 5 A fuse with a 20 A fuse:** excessive current could cause a fire.
* **Fuse or breaker goes in series with the hot conductor only.**
* **Lightning arrester:** on a **grounded panel** near where feed lines enter the building.
* **Ground rods:** **bond them all together** with heavy wire or strap.
* **Power supply right after turning off:** charge stored in **filter capacitors.**
* **Measuring high voltage:** be sure the meter and leads are rated for it.

## T0B · Towers and antennas

* **Never** climb a tower without a helper or observer.
* **Stay clear of overhead power lines.** If the antenna falls, no part may come within **10 feet** of the power line.
* **Never attach an antenna to a utility pole:** the antenna could contact high-voltage power lines.
* **Turnbuckle safety wire** prevents loosening from vibration.
* **Crank-up towers:** do not climb unless retracted, or mechanical safety locking devices are installed.
* **Grounding:** separate **8-foot ground rods** per leg, bonded to the tower and each other. Keep connections **short and direct**; avoid **sharp bends**. **Local electrical codes** set requirements.
* **Climbing requires all three:** sufficient training in safe tower-climbing technique, appropriate tie-off to the tower at all times, and an approved climbing harness. Also, never climb without a helper or observer.

<!-- img:tower Tower work. Harness, tie-off, training and a spotter on the ground. -->

## T0C · RF exposure

* Radio signals are **non-ionizing radiation**. RF does not have enough energy to cause chemical changes in cells or damage DNA, unlike ionizing radiation such as X-rays and radioactivity. (RF can still heat tissue, which is why exposure limits exist.)
* **Why limits depend on frequency:** the human body absorbs more RF energy at some frequencies than at others.
* **50 MHz** has the **lowest** permissible exposure of the listed bands (that is where the body absorbs RF most efficiently at the lower VHF range).
* **Duty cycle:** percentage of time a transmitter is transmitting. **If duty cycle drops from 100% to 50%, allowable power density increases by a factor of 2.**
* **Factors in RF exposure (the pool lists all three):** frequency and power level of the RF field, distance from the antenna to a person, and the antenna's radiation pattern. Duty cycle matters too, because it sets the *average* exposure.
* **Acceptable ways to check compliance (all three):** calculation based on FCC OET Bulletin 65, calculation based on computer modeling, and measurement of field strength with calibrated equipment.
* **Reduce exposure:** relocate antennas, reduce power, reduce duty cycle.
* **RF burn:** the hazard of touching an antenna during transmission.
* **Re-evaluate the station** whenever you change anything in the transmitter or antenna system.
* **The station licensee** is responsible for ensuring no one is exposed above the FCC limits.
